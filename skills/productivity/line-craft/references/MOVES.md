# The moves: prior art

Where each move in `../SKILL.md` comes from. Source study: all fifteen LucasArts adventures
(1986-2000), read by research agents in October 2026 and independently audited (medium-high
trust). The full cited findings live in the writing workspace:
`D:\code\writing\_spikes\research\lucasarts-dialogue-findings.md`, with its audit next to it. This
summary paraphrases; it quotes no game text.

| Move | What the games do | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Reply or act** | The SCUMM games have no narrator, so a line's meaning always rests on the next speaker or the next action. Strong sincere exchanges move from line to reply to plot, never to interpretation. | Monkey Island 1, Full Throttle, Grim Fandango |
| **Puncture** | When a character states the lesson, the next speaker turns it into a joke or contradicts it with a fact. Loom's original script refused a request in the other speaker's own words; its later revision replaced that with a line stating the theme, and critics count the revision as worse. | Monkey Island 1, Fate of Atlantis, Day of the Tentacle, Loom |
| **Errand** | A dying character asks to do one more thing, and within a few lines the scene is a practical puzzle. Horror is answered with a technical fact about what can and can't be done. | Grim Fandango, The Dig, Curse of Monkey Island |
| **Hedge** | A love declaration is aimed at someone else, as a rejection; the answer to "did you mean it" hesitates; a direct question about pain is answered about loyalty. Every sincere beat is interrupted by business. | Curse of Monkey Island, Grim Fandango |
| **Literal act** | Menace is specific and domestic: a curse fails because of shoddy materials, a monster's nature comes out as a complaint about missed meals. Tim Schafer describes showing backstory through "a slit in the fence". | Monkey Island 2, Maniac Mansion, Grim Fandango (Schafer, 2012) |
| **Literal-first** | A dark line works when it is first a plain prediction; an alien's chiasmic maxims about death fail, and reviewers called that game's dialogue cheesy. The one wise closer that works is a reply to a direct question, built from the story's own world. | The Dig, Grim Fandango |

Supporting craft notes:
- **Shrink as the stakes grow.** The bigger the catastrophe, the shorter and flatter the response.
  (Day of the Tentacle, Full Throttle)
- **Change the narrating voice, not the material.** Zak McKracken's first design was "too serious";
  the team kept the material and made the hero a tabloid reporter (David Fox).
- **Lines that don't sell.** Sam & Max was cast for a voice that wasn't "trying too hard to sell the
  lines". In prose, a line should work read flat.
- **Choices as characterization.** An overwrought option can sit on the menu for another character
  to judge; every branch gets a real payoff. (Monkey Island 2, Curse)

Warnings from the same corpus:
- Deflation without warmth reads as contempt (critics on Monkey Island 2).
- A callback that points at the story's own earlier line, asking to be admired, is the comedy form
  of the narrator gloss (reviewers on Escape from Monkey Island).
- Mythic register belongs to a character whose speech is always mythic; the same line from a dry
  narrator is portentous.
- Revision can make the theme more explicit, not less (Loom); shortening alone is not a fix.

The dividing line: sincere scenes land when they are about **a specific promise, task or want**,
and go portentous when a character **explains what the event means**.
